# Safeguards, pause rules, and failure thresholds

**Version:** 0.1 public working safeguards  
**Status:** Current candidate for external review. These rules are not legal advice, a risk assessment for a specific site, ethics approval, safeguarding certification, or a substitute for competent local review.

## 1. Governing rule

Pilot A must be able to fail safely and informatively.

“Successful” attendance, activity, or contribution does not excuse coercion, unsafe practice, exclusion, inaccessible design, hidden labour, facilitator capture, unlawful work, or data misuse. Failure evidence is a result, not an embarrassment to conceal.

## 2. Hard gates before recruitment

All seven gates must be cleared for the actual site and jurisdiction. An unresolved gate means **do not recruit**.

| Gate | Required determination | Minimum evidence before recruitment |
|---|---|---|
| G1 Host authority | A lawful host and named decision-maker can authorise, supervise, pause, and stop the pilot | Written host approval; role and escalation map |
| G2 Insurance and liability | Activities, people, venue, equipment, and facilitator roles are within appropriate cover | Written confirmation from competent host/insurer or adviser |
| G3 Work health and safety | Hazards, controls, incident response, competence, and regulator obligations are site-specific | Reviewed risk register, emergency process, activity boundaries |
| G4 Volunteer/employment | Required work is correctly classified and paid where an employment relationship exists | Role map, task/time expectations, competent employment review |
| G5 Conduct and safeguarding | Harassment, violence, sexual harm, exploitation, complaints, and vulnerable-person risks have usable controls | Code/process, named safeguarding route, screening where required |
| G6 Accessibility | Physical, sensory, communication, cognitive, social, transport, timing, and cost barriers have been reviewed | Access walk-through and lived-experience review; adjustment process |
| G7 Data and privacy | Collection is minimal, lawful, fair, transparent, secured, retained briefly, and deletable where required | Host-specific data map, notice/consent, access/retention/breach process |

## 3. Immediate stop conditions

Stop the affected activity or the pilot immediately when any of the following occurs or is credibly alleged:

1. serious or imminent danger, serious injury, or uncontrolled hazard;
2. credible threat or act of violence;
3. sexual harm, predation, grooming, or serious harassment;
4. exploitation of a child, vulnerable person, participant, volunteer, or worker;
5. a serious privacy, confidentiality, security, or consent breach;
6. activity outside the reviewed competence, insurance, legal, or risk scope;
7. discovery that the pilot lacks the authority, insurance, or legal basis required to operate;
8. coercion tied to food, shelter, essential support, benefits, employment, safety, or another necessity; or
9. inability to provide the minimum facilitator, safeguarding, or emergency coverage.

Stopping is protective, not a finding of guilt. Follow the host's emergency, reporting, preservation, support, and referral obligations. Do not investigate beyond competence or promise confidentiality that law or safety duties do not permit.

Restart requires documented review by the person or body with relevant authority and competence. Some events may make restart inappropriate.

## 4. Pause-and-redesign conditions

These conditions may be detected through observation, complaints, interviews, exit cards, facilitator logs, or non-use. Pause the affected mechanism when the pattern is credible; do not wait for a numerical threshold that the small pilot cannot justify.

| ID | Condition | Examples of signal | Required response before restart |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Contribution pressure | People apologise for “doing nothing”; facilitators repeatedly ask what someone can offer; receiving creates debt | Restate and demonstrate unconditional participation; remove prompts or rewards causing pressure; test refusal again |
| P2 | Capacity capture | A post becomes an assignment, label, expectation, or repeated request | Remove/expire entry; stop matching; confirm consent and no penalty; redesign controls |
| P3 | Contributor hierarchy | Visible helpers receive better welcome, influence, access, or praise than quiet participants | Stop status-producing recognition; review access/rule decisions; restore equal-standing practice |
| P4 | Hidden roster | The same people become expected cleaners, hosts, cooks, carers, or organisers | Resource, simplify, rotate only by genuine opt-in, or pay; do not rely on guilt |
| P5 | Facilitator overcentralisation | Most relationships require one broker; facilitator preferences shape access or recognition | Add supervision, shared decisions, conflict disclosure, alternative contact routes, or reduce scope |
| P6 | Accessibility failure | A nominal option is unusable because of venue, sensory load, communication, timing, transport, cost, or social format | Review with affected people; provide adjustment or alternative; do not treat nonattendance as disinterest |
| P7 | Compulsory intimacy | Participation requires personal disclosure, group introduction, emotional sharing, or unwanted contact | Reinstate private, quiet, and non-disclosing paths; remove the requirement |
| P8 | Deservingness drift | Access or welcome turns on perceived effort, gratitude, charisma, capacity, or contribution | Separate conduct/risk decisions from human standing; review decisions and language |
| P9 | Mission substitution | The pilot starts replacing a public, professional, regulated, clinical, care, legal, or crisis service | Stop the substituted activity; refer appropriately; narrow scope |
| P10 | Data creep | Records become durable profiles, social graphs, sensitive narratives, performance data, or material for unrelated use | Stop collection/use; secure and delete where appropriate; perform privacy review and re-consent if lawful |

## 5. Cross-cutting tests

### Refusal test

At any invitation, ask: can the person say no, give no reason, remain welcome, retain unconditional access, and avoid repeated persuasion? If not, the offer is not voluntary enough for Pilot A.

### Noncontributor test

Compare the welcome, attention, information, and access experienced by someone who posts or helps with someone who does neither. A persistent gap is a design failure even if contributors report high satisfaction.

### Founder/facilitator power test

Ask who can define success, interpret complaints, grant access, broker introductions, hold data, approve expenses, change rules, and represent the project. Concentration without independent review is a pause signal.

### Hidden-labour test

List every task required before, during, and after a session. If the plan depends on work that is unnamed, unresourced, or repeatedly absorbed by the same people, redesign before continuation.

### Accessibility test

Ask whether a person can understand the invitation, reach and enter the site, regulate social and sensory exposure, communicate by an available method, decline or leave, and participate without unaffordable cost. “Open to everyone” is not evidence of access.

### Economic-membrane test

Review any substantial, repeated, specialist, risky, scheduled, commercially valuable, essential, or accountable activity. Do not solve the boundary by calling required work a gift.

### Exit test

A participant can leave the activity or pilot without having to justify the decision, repay help, surrender unrelated access, or accept follow-up. Safety or legal records may still need to be retained under the host-specific policy.

## 6. Informative failure categories

The following are valid findings:

- the discovery board is unused or confusing;
- informal conversation works better than the board;
- people feel observed or assigned despite the controls;
- low-social-intensity and high-social-intensity needs cannot be met in one format;
- confident or articulate participants gain disproportionate visibility;
- facilitator burden is too high or relationships are broker-dependent;
- accessibility changes alter the mechanism enough to require a different design;
- reciprocal norms become debt even without a formal ledger;
- the host cannot resource the unconditional layer without participant labour;
- existing community institutions provide the same benefit more safely or simply; or
- the small design cannot answer the stated question.

None should be relabelled as participant resistance or poor engagement to protect the theory.

## 7. Threshold policy

This version deliberately sets no arbitrary percentage for belonging, pressure, board use, return, or connection. With approximately 10–30 participants, percentages can imply precision the design does not possess and can hide important minority harm.

Before a live pilot, an independent methods reviewer should define:

- which events require immediate action regardless of frequency;
- how repeated qualitative signals trigger pause;
- how missing responses and early exits are interpreted;
- how subgroup and accessibility concerns can be detected without exposing identity;
- what continuation decision the sample can and cannot support; and
- how adverse, null, and contradictory results will be reported.

## 8. Minimal incident and decision record

For a stop or pause, record only what the host process requires:

- date and mechanism affected;
- stop/pause category;
- immediate protective action;
- who held decision authority;
- required reporting or referral completed;
- data access and confidentiality limits;
- restart, redesign, or closure decision and rationale; and
- follow-up owner.

Do not place identifiable incident details in this public repository or an open issue.

## 9. No-success-at-any-cost rule

The pilot does not continue merely because participants enjoy it, funders like it, the founder is invested in it, media attention appears, or activity metrics rise. A Commons that produces connection by hiding coercion, hierarchy, exclusion, risk, or unpaid essential work has not met its own test.
