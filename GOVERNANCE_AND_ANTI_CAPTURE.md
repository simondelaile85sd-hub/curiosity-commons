# Governance and anti-capture

**Status:** Current project synthesis. The normative constraints are part of the present architecture; the mechanisms are candidates until externally reviewed, legally situated, and tested. No corporate, cooperative, trust, membership, ownership, or financial structure has been adopted.

## 1. Why anti-capture is part of the design

A Curiosity Commons could appear busy, generous, or popular while becoming the opposite of its purpose. Capture may occur through property ownership, money, founder control, facilitation, informal friendship networks, expertise, data access, meeting attendance, contributor status, or dependence on a funder or host.

The aim is not to pretend hierarchy can be eliminated. It is to make power legible, bounded, contestable, and unable to convert achievement or contribution into superior human standing.

## 2. Current normative constraints

The project currently treats these as constraints on later governance design:

- standing precedes productivity;
- contribution is voluntary;
- refusal and exit are legitimate;
- quiet and noncontributing participation remain real membership modes;
- recognition does not automatically confer rule-making power;
- curiosity does not create entitlement to another person's knowledge, story, body, labour, or culture;
- accessibility is part of usable freedom;
- community language does not replace safeguarding or due process;
- commerce supports rather than defines the Commons;
- AI may suggest only within explicit human governance and must not rank human worth;
- conduct, competence, consent, risk, and scarcity may justify specific boundaries; and
- dissent must not be reframed as failure to contribute.

These commitments are normative project choices. Their durability under real pressure is untested.

## 3. Power map

Governance review should distinguish at least seven forms of power:

| Power domain | Typical holder | Capture question |
|---|---|---|
| Resource | property owner, funder, equipment holder | Can support be withdrawn to change the mission or silence critique? |
| Rule-making | board, founder, host, active meeting group | Who can change belonging, access, exchange, or data rules? |
| Administrative | facilitator, roster keeper, platform operator | Who controls invitations, schedules, visibility, and exceptions? |
| Adjudicative and appeal | complaints role, host, committee | Is there a route independent of the person complained about? |
| Informational | data custodian, storyteller, researcher | Who knows what about whom, and who controls interpretation? |
| Social and status | charismatic, articulate, connected, high-output participants | Does visibility become belonging, access, or authority? |
| Economic and dependency | employer, buyer, sponsor, service provider | Can income, essentials, or opportunity be conditioned on compliance? |

No mature design should concentrate all seven domains in the founder, host, property owner, or facilitation team.

## 4. Candidate governance mechanisms

Possible mechanisms include elected roles, rotation, sortition, affected-person representation, conflict declarations, term limits, recall, an independent complaints or ombuds route, supermajorities for constitutional changes, published decision reasons, and subsidiarity between local sites and any federation.

These are options, not a constitution. Each can produce new exclusion: elections favour recognition, rotation can burden people, sortition without access support can be nominal, supermajorities can entrench a bloc, and formal complaints processes can be inaccessible or unsafe. Mechanisms require lived review and clear resourcing.

## 5. Belonging, governance, and ownership are distinct

The project does not assume that unconditional belonging gives every person identical authority over every legal, financial, safety, or scarce-resource decision. It also does not assume that investment, employment, property, or high contribution gives superior standing.

Future design must separate:

1. **belonging and participation** — the unconditional social layer;
2. **governance membership and delegated authority** — bounded decisions with accountability;
3. **capital, income, or return rights** — explicit economic arrangements; and
4. **asset stewardship and mission protection** — the legal control of property and purpose.

Possible vehicles—cooperative, mutual, trust, incorporated association, nonprofit company, asset lock, or hybrid—remain unselected and require jurisdiction-specific advice.

## 6. Recognition without domination

Recognition can support learning and gratitude, but durable recognition can become rank. Monitor whether:

- the same people receive public praise;
- visible output eclipses care, welcome, maintenance, or quiet support;
- expertise becomes general authority outside its domain;
- a contributor gains better access to scarce activities;
- recognised participants dominate meetings or represent the project externally;
- informal friendship with facilitators changes opportunities; or
- “founding” status becomes permanent veto power.

Recognition should be specific, time-bounded, contestable where it affects access, and separated from rule-making by default.

## 7. Hidden labour and contributor hierarchy

Every operating cycle should map preparation, welcome, access work, cleaning, food, documentation, emotional labour, safeguarding, conflict handling, follow-up, and close-down. Track who carries those tasks and whether they are paid, formally volunteered, rotated, simplified, or merely assumed.

A capacity ceiling is a proposed protective idea: when an individual is giving beyond a sustainable or equitable level, the system should reduce dependency, redistribute, resource, or stop the work rather than celebrate the overextension. The ceiling must not require medical proof or intrusive capacity surveillance.

A solidarity buffer is another proposal: asymmetrical receiving can be legitimate without creating individual debt. It remains a design hypothesis, not an accounting mechanism in Pilot A.

## 8. Economic membrane and mission drift

The economic membrane separates belonging from formal economic obligation. Governance should identify:

- which activities must be paid or professionally held;
- who decides when a gift has become required work;
- whether commerce creates preferential access or decision power;
- whether a funder metric displaces the project's actual safeguards;
- whether essential infrastructure depends on a small unpaid group;
- whether products or services extract from community knowledge, stories, or relationships; and
- how income, surplus, intellectual contribution, and asset value are governed.

No claim is made that a membrane alone prevents mission drift. It is a review boundary whose legal and institutional form is unresolved.

## 9. Data and AI power

Pilot A is manual-first. A later AI layer is not presumed.

Before any participant-facing algorithm is considered, the project would need evidence that:

- a real coordination problem remains after manual learning;
- the data required can be collected without coercion or profiling;
- participants can understand, decline, correct, and delete relevant data;
- ranking, inferred vulnerability, and hidden optimisation are prohibited and auditable;
- human facilitators do not treat suggestions as authority;
- appeal and non-digital paths are usable; and
- benefits exceed surveillance, bias, and dependency costs.

AI must not decide belonging, worth, deservingness, governance power, or access to essentials.

## 10. Story and cultural rights boundary

Earlier work explored a possible Story Commons and comparative story ecology. That material is parked. Personal stories are not a free input to community design, publication, transcription, AI analysis, or training. Anonymity alone does not resolve privacy, cultural authority, collective rights, withdrawal, or future-use questions.

Any later story work requires a separate consent and rights architecture. Work involving Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander peoples, knowledge, or cultural material requires appropriate Indigenous leadership, collaboration, authority, and ethics processes; it cannot be operationalised from this repository alone.

## 11. Hostile-adoption tests

Before adopting or scaling a design, ask:

1. Could a property owner call a tenant programme a Commons while retaining unilateral control?
2. Could a funder require contribution metrics that make belonging conditional?
3. Could a founder, clique, or articulate minority dominate informal decisions?
4. Could facilitators allocate opportunity to friends or high-status contributors?
5. Could “capacity discovery” become employability screening or a labour pool?
6. Could volunteers displace paid workers or professional services?
7. Could a buyer extract commercial value from unpaid community work?
8. Could access or safety language hide prejudice or retaliation?
9. Could local autonomy protect abuse from external scrutiny?
10. Could federation centralise property, data, identity, or standards?
11. Could recognition become a durable contributor class?
12. Could AI turn provisional self-description into a permanent profile?
13. Could participant stories be monetised, archived, or trained on beyond consent?
14. Could quiet participants disappear from evaluation because they produce less data?
15. Could accessibility be promised rhetorically while transport, timing, cost, or social intensity remain prohibitive?
16. Could the language of capacity-relative contribution become surveillance of illness, disability, care, or poverty?

A credible “yes” does not always require abandonment, but it requires a named control, independent review, and a stop path.

## 12. Scale and replication

Pilot A is local and single-site. Federation, replication, enterprise, and shared ownership are later proposals.

Any later model should avoid a single critical facilitator, keep recoverable operational knowledge, distinguish local adaptation from constitutional erosion, and make it easier—not mandatory—for another Commons to learn from prior work. Replication must not imply franchising, certification, or authority to use the project name.

## 13. Current governance conclusion

The present work is strong enough to specify what a future governance design must protect and what forms of capture to test. It is not strong enough to choose a legal entity, allocate ownership, authorise a host, claim democratic legitimacy, or certify an implementation as Curiosity Commons.
